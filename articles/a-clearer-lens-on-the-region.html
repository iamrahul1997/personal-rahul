Nepal · Explained

A clearer lens on the region we call home

By Rahul Poudel May 2026 7 min read

Most of what the world reads about South Asia is written from somewhere else. The frames arrive pre-assembled: a rivalry to be managed, a market to be unlocked, a buffer to be watched. Nepal, when it appears at all, is usually a sentence in someone else's paragraph.

This essay is an argument for flipping the map—for starting the story of the region from Kathmandu and seeing what changes.

What the standard frame misses

Seen from outside, the Himalaya is a wall between giants. Seen from here, it is a corridor of rivers, remittances, pilgrimage routes, and power lines. The Nepal–India border is one of the most intensely lived frontiers on earth: millions of crossings, marriages, and market days that no geopolitical briefing captures. Our relationship with China is likewise more textured than the transit-and-tunnels story suggests.

Regions look different from their center than from their edges—and for us, Kathmandu is the center.

When analysis starts from lived geography rather than strategic abstraction, different questions surface. Not "which side is Nepal on?" but: who benefits from our hydropower contracts? What do our migration corridors owe the workers who sustain them? Which regional institutions actually deliver for small members?

Building our own analytical tradition

A clearer lens is not just a rhetorical preference; it requires infrastructure. Nepal needs think tanks that publish in Nepali and English, datasets that outlive news cycles, and a habit of primary-source reading—treaties, budgets, loan agreements—rather than secondhand commentary.

None of this is beyond us. The raw material—curious students, a free press, a diaspora fluent in the world's institutions—already exists. What's missing is the confidence to treat our own vantage point as a serious place to think from. That confidence is the quiet project of this site.
