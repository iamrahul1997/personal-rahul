Technology is changing diplomacy — are we paying attention?

By Rahul Poudel June 2026 6 min read

When we picture diplomacy, we still picture rooms: summits, handshakes, communiqués. But an increasing share of statecraft now happens in systems—payment rails, undersea cables, satellite constellations, and the standards bodies that decide how they all talk to each other.

This shift matters enormously for a country like Nepal, because the entry ticket to technological statecraft is not an aircraft carrier. It is competence.

The new instruments of influence

Consider what has changed in a decade. India exported not just software but an entire model of digital public infrastructure—identity, payments, data exchange—that dozens of countries are now adapting. Export controls on semiconductors have become the sharpest edge of great-power competition. And foreign ministries from Singapore to Denmark have appointed tech ambassadors whose counterparts are companies, not countries.

The map of power now includes server farms and standards committees alongside straits and mountain passes.

None of this fits neatly into the diplomacy we teach or practice. Trade officials negotiate data-flow clauses they did not draft. Security officials assess technologies they cannot audit. The gap between what governments must decide and what they understand is widening everywhere—but it punishes small states most, because we have the least room for expensive mistakes.

Where Nepal should focus

Nepal does not need to win the AI race. It needs three humbler things. First, genuine technical literacy inside government—people who can read a cloud contract or a spectrum allocation with professional skepticism. Second, a clear position on digital sovereignty: what data about Nepalis should be governed by Nepali law, and how. Third, active participation in the forums where rules are written, from ICANN to the ITU, where a prepared small delegation can punch far above its weight.

The countries that shape the next order will be the ones fluent in both languages—power and protocol. There is no reason Nepal cannot be among the fluent. But fluency is a choice, and it begins with paying attention.
