Why Nepal's voice matters in a multipolar world

By Rahul Poudel July 2026 8 min read

For most of the past three decades, the story of international politics had a simple plot: one superpower, one set of rules, one direction of travel. Small states like Nepal learned to navigate that world by keeping their heads down and their options few. That world is gone.

What has replaced it is messier and, paradoxically, more open. Power is diffusing—across capitals, corporations, and coalitions. For a country wedged between two giants, this is usually described as a danger. I want to make the opposite case: the multipolar moment is the best opportunity Nepal has had in a generation to matter.

Small is not the same as silent

The instinct to treat smallness as weakness runs deep in our foreign-policy conversation. But look around: Singapore turned geography into leverage. Qatar turned mediation into influence. Estonia turned digital governance into a brand that outweighs its population a hundredfold. None of these countries waited for permission to be relevant.

Influence in this era is less about the size of your army and more about the clarity of your position.

Nepal has assets these countries would envy: a location at the seam of the world's two most consequential rising powers, a large and young diaspora, and a genuine tradition of non-alignment that predates the current fashion for "strategic autonomy."

The cost of having no position

The risk for Nepal is not choosing the wrong side. It is being seen as a country that has no considered position at all—a place where policy is improvised transaction by transaction. When your stance is unpredictable, partners discount you. When it is principled and consistent, even great powers must factor you in.

That consistency has to be built at home first: in parliament, in universities, in the press, and in conversations like this one. Foreign policy is too important to remain the private language of a small circle in Kathmandu.

What an engaged generation can do

This is where young Nepalis come in. The generation now entering public life is the most globally connected in our history. The task is to convert that connection into competence: learn the region's languages, study the supply chains, read the treaties, and then argue—publicly and well—about what Nepal should want.

A multipolar world rewards countries that know their own mind. Nepal's voice matters, but only if we do the unglamorous work of deciding what it should say.
